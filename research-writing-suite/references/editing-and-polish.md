# Editing & Polish

**Owns:** improving text the user already wrote: line editing, tightening, clarity, flow,
tone and register, consistency, cutting to a word limit, fixing "AI-sounding" prose, and
executing a revision plan.
**Use when:** "polish this", "make it more professional/academic", "tighten to 250 words",
"improve flow", "does this sound natural?", "fix grammar", "apply my supervisor's comments".
**Don't use when:** the user wants a judgment of the methods, evidence, or argument
(`peer-review-and-critique.md`), new text from scratch (`academic-writing.md` or
`professional-reports.md`), or citation formatting (`citations.md`).

## 1. Autonomy rule (suite standard)

- **Apply directly:** spelling, grammar, punctuation, typos, consistency (terms, numbers, tense,
  abbreviations), and wordiness that doesn't change meaning.
- **Flag first, then change:** anything that alters meaning, the strength of a claim, the
  argument's structure, the order of sections, or adds or removes content. List these and ask
  "Which should I apply?" unless the user said "rewrite freely" or "go ahead with everything".
- **Preserve the author's voice:** keep their vocabulary and rhythm where it works, and their
  spelling variety (US/UK).
- **Never add facts or citations while editing.** If a claim needs support, flag it with
  `[CITATION NEEDED]`.

## 2. Edit levels

Ask which level the user wants if it isn't clear from the request; default to **line edit**.

| Level | Scope |
|---|---|
| **Proofread** | Errors only: spelling, grammar, punctuation, typos, formatting |
| **Copy edit** | Plus consistency, style-guide conformity, and the clearest word choice |
| **Line edit** (default) | Plus sentence clarity, concision, flow, transitions, and tone |
| **Structural edit** | Plus paragraph and section organisation and argument flow (flag before applying) |

## 3. Issue scan

Before rewriting anything substantial, scan for:

**Clarity and precision**
- Vague quantifiers ("many studies", "often") → number them or cite.
- Undefined terms or abbreviations; inconsistent terms for the same thing.
- Nominalisations ("conducted an investigation of" → "investigated").
- Long sentences with stacked clauses. Aim for 15–25 words on average, with varied length.

**Logic and flow**
- A topic sentence that doesn't match its paragraph; paragraphs doing two jobs.
- Transitions that assert a link the text doesn't build ("Therefore…" without a reason).
- Old-to-new information order broken (start sentences with known information, end with new).

**Calibration**
- Overclaiming ("proves", "clearly demonstrates") or causal language on correlational evidence.
- Over-hedging ("it could perhaps be argued that this may suggest").

**Concision**
- Throat-clearing ("It is important to note that"), redundancy ("future plans", "end result"),
  and filler ("in order to" → "to").

**Mechanics and consistency**
- Numbers and units, tense, capitalisation of headings, list parallelism, and figure/table references.

## 4. Showing changes

Choose by length:
- **Short text (≤ 300 words):** give the clean revised version, then a bullet list of the main
  changes with one-line reasons.
- **Longer text or substantive edits:** show changes inline, then the clean version:
  `~~original phrasing~~ → **revised phrasing** (reason)`
- **Whole documents:** a table of the top issues (location · issue · fix), then the revised text
  section by section.

Keep explanations short. The reasons teach the writer what to fix next time.

## 5. Tightening and word limits

To cut, work in this order (most words saved, least meaning lost):
1. Remove repetition across sentences and paragraphs (keep the best instance).
2. Cut throat-clearing, signposting fluff, and redundant examples.
3. Merge sentences that share a subject.
4. Convert nominalisations and passive constructions where the actor matters.
5. Replace phrases with words ("due to the fact that" → "because").
6. Only then trim content, and flag any content cut.

Report the word count before and after.

## 6. Tone and register

| Target | Moves |
|---|---|
| **Academic** | Precise terms, hedges calibrated to evidence, no contractions or colloquialisms, impersonal or field-normal "we", citations integrated into sentences |
| **Professional / business** | Plain words, short sentences, active voice, BLUF, specific asks, no hype |
| **Technical** | Exact terminology, defined acronyms, numbers with units, imperative mood for procedures |
| **Public / lay** | Everyday words, explain jargon with an example, one idea per sentence, concrete stakes |
| **Persuasive** | Claim → evidence → benefit to the reader; address the main objection; strong verbs; no overclaiming |
| **Email or letter** | Purpose in the first line, polite but direct, the ask with a deadline, a short close |

When changing tone, keep the content identical unless the user asks otherwise.

## 7. Removing AI-sounding prose

Readers distrust text that sounds generated. Detect and fix these patterns, whoever wrote the text:
- **Inflated openers and closers:** "In today's rapidly evolving landscape…", "In conclusion, it is
  clear that…". Start with the point; end with the implication.
- **Empty intensifiers and buzzwords:** "crucial", "pivotal", "delve", "tapestry", "navigate the
  complexities", "a testament to", "unlock", "leverage", "robust", "seamless", "game-changer".
  Use plain specific words, or delete them.
- **Rule-of-three padding:** reflexive triplets ("efficient, effective, and scalable"). Keep only
  the attributes you can support.
- **Hedged balance without a position:** "While X has benefits, it also has drawbacks." Say which
  matters more and why.
- **Uniform rhythm:** every sentence the same length and shape. Vary the length; let important
  sentences be short.
- **Over-signposting:** "It is worth noting", "Importantly,", "Additionally," at the start of most
  sentences. Cut most of them.
- **Generic examples:** replace them with the user's actual case, data, or a cited instance.
- **Bullet-itis:** fragments where reasoning is needed. Convert them to connected prose.
- **Em-dash and colon overuse:** vary the punctuation.

The goal is clear, specific, human writing, not "evading detection". Never help disguise
work in a way that breaks an academic-integrity or AI-use policy. If the user's context
requires disclosure of AI assistance, remind them.

## 8. Abstract and short-text polish

For abstracts, bios, cover letters, and summaries, check the following:
1. Does the first sentence state the specific problem or purpose?
2. Are the aim, method, main result (with a number), and conclusion all present?
3. Is the conclusion within the evidence?
4. Have undefined abbreviations, citations (unless allowed), and filler been cut?
5. Is the word limit met (report the count)?

Offer a **cautious** and an **assertive** version of the final sentence when claim strength is the
open question. The structure for writing an abstract from scratch is in `academic-writing.md` §5.

## 9. Executing a revision plan

When the user has supervisor, reviewer, or editor comments (the plan may come from
`academic-writing.md` §7 or `peer-review-and-critique.md` §6):
1. Confirm the change table (number, priority, type, location, action).
2. Apply changes in priority order. Keep a log: `# | location | what changed`.
3. If a change conflicts with another comment or with the evidence, stop and flag it. Don't
   silently choose.
4. Re-check consistency after structural changes (numbers, terms, cross-references, abstract
   vs. body).
