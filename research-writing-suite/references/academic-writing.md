# Academic Writing

**Owns:** drafting new academic text: paper sections (IMRaD and alternatives), thesis and
dissertation chapters, essays, abstracts, research and grant proposals, conference and poster
abstracts, cover letters to editors, and AI-use or other declarations.
**Use when:** "write my introduction", "draft a methods section", "write an abstract for…", "help
me write a research proposal", "outline my thesis chapter".
**Don't use when:**
- The user has text and wants it improved (`editing-and-polish.md`).
- They want it judged (`peer-review-and-critique.md`).
- It's a business or technical report (`professional-reports.md`).
- It's a standalone literature review (`literature-review.md`, which owns its method; this file's
  §4 covers only the review *section* inside a paper).

## 1. Before drafting

Settle these, inferring and stating defaults:
- **Genre and venue:** journal article, thesis, course essay, proposal, conference? Is there a
  template, word limit, or required structure?
- **Audience:** specialists, an interdisciplinary panel, a teacher or marker, a committee?
- **Core claim:** the one-sentence contribution or thesis. If the user can't state it, help them
  find it first (ask "What should a reader believe after reading this that they didn't before?").
- **Materials:** the user's notes, data, results, sources, and outline. Draft only from these plus
  verified sources. Never invent results, sample sizes, statistics, approvals, or citations; use
  `[DATA NEEDED]` and `[CITATION NEEDED]`.
- **Citation style:** per `citations.md` (default APA 7).

## 2. The paragraph method

Build academic prose from **atomic claims**: one claim per line, each paired with its source.

> Working memory capacity predicts reading comprehension (Author, Year).
> The effect is weaker in older adults (Author, Year).
> No study has tested this in second-language readers [gap; supported by search date].

Then:
1. State the paragraph's **function** (define, establish, contrast, show gap, justify method,
   interpret result…).
2. Write a **topic sentence** that makes a specific claim. It must not announce a topic.
   - Weak: "Cognitive load has been widely studied."
   - Strong: "Cognitive load limits learning when instruction exceeds working-memory capacity."
3. Develop it: explanation, then evidence woven into the sentences (comparing sources, not listing
   them), then qualification.
4. Close with an implication or a link to the next paragraph.
5. **One idea per paragraph.** If two functions appear, split it.

If the user asks for academic text without sources, write a draft but mark every factual claim
`[UNCITED — needs source]`, and tell them the draft isn't defensible until the claims are anchored.

**Claim strength:** draft at the strength the evidence supports. For key sentences (thesis
statements, abstract conclusions, contribution claims), you may offer two calibrated
alternatives, **cautious** and **assertive**, when the user is unsure how strongly to claim.

## 3. Argument structure

- **Claim → reasons → evidence → warrant → counterargument → rebuttal or qualification.**
  Make the warrant (why the evidence supports the claim) explicit when it isn't obvious.
- Put evidence **before or with** the claims it supports, never after the conclusion.
- Address the strongest counterargument, not a weak one.
- Signpost with logic, not filler: "Because X, Y" or "However, Z challenges this", not
  "Furthermore" stacked on unrelated points.

## 4. Section guides

**Introduction (empirical paper):** (1) the specific problem and stakes, not a generic opener;
(2) what is known, briefly synthesised; (3) the gap: what is unknown or contested, and why that
matters; (4) this study: aim, question or hypotheses, approach; (5) the contribution; (6) an
optional one-line roadmap. Common failures: a broad opening ("Since ancient times…"), a gap
without consequence, and the contribution buried late.

**Literature-review section (inside a paper or thesis):** organise by theme; end each strand by
connecting it to the present study; synthesise and compare rather than list; include contrary
evidence. For a full review method use `literature-review.md`.

**Methods:** enough detail to replicate. Cover design; participants or data (N, recruitment,
criteria, descriptives); measures (with reliability and validity); procedure; ethics approval and
consent (as provided, never invented); analysis plan (software and version, models,
pre-specified vs. exploratory, missing data, multiplicity). Write what was done, in the past tense.
Make agency clear ("participants were randomly assigned by software…").

**Results:** report without interpreting. Follow the order of the questions or hypotheses. Give
complete statistics (test, df, statistic, p, **effect size, CI**), and report null and unexpected
results. Text summarises tables; it doesn't repeat them. Every table and figure is referenced in
the text and has units, n, and uncertainty.

**Discussion:** (1) the main findings in plain words, with no new statistics; (2) interpretation
per finding, linked to the literature from the introduction; (3) unexpected and null findings,
addressed directly; (4) specific limitations, each with its likely effect on the conclusions; (5)
implications that match the evidence; (6) concrete future work; (7) a brief conclusion. Avoid
both overclaiming ("proves") and hedging valid findings into meaninglessness.

**Qualitative variations:** add the researcher's positionality or reflexivity; the sampling and
saturation rationale; the analytic approach (e.g., reflexive thematic analysis), coding procedure,
and trustworthiness strategies; and present themes with illustrative quotes.

**Course essay (argumentative or analytical):** an introduction that ends in a **thesis
statement** (an arguable claim plus its main reasons) → body paragraphs, one reason each
(claim, evidence with citation, analysis of *how* the evidence supports the claim, link back to
the thesis) → a counterargument and response → a conclusion that answers "so what?" without new
evidence. Check the assignment brief and rubric first, and map each rubric criterion to where the
essay meets it. The analysis should outweigh the summary.

**Alternative structures:** theoretical and argumentative papers, case studies, design or
engineering papers (problem → requirements → design → evaluation), and humanities essays
(thesis-driven sections). Use IMRaD only when it fits.

## 5. Abstracts (all kinds)

Write the abstract **last**, from the finished text. It must contain nothing absent from the paper.

**Structured (empirical):** Background/Objective (1–2 sentences) → Methods (design, sample,
measures; 2–3 sentences) → Results (main findings with key numbers; 2–3 sentences) →
Conclusions (implication matched to the evidence; 1–2 sentences).

**Unstructured:** the same content as flowing prose. Typically 150–250 words; check the limit.

**Other abstract types:**
- *Conference or poster abstract:* lead with the finding; one key figure-worthy number; say why the
  audience should come; respect strict limits (often 200–300 words).
- *Thesis abstract:* the question, approach, main contributions (often numbered), significance.
- *Graphical abstract or lay summary:* plain language, no jargon, one main message, and what it
  means for the reader.
- *Proposal abstract or project summary:* problem, aims, approach, expected outcomes, impact.

**Abstract polish checks:** first sentence specific; aim explicit; method named; numbers present
where results exist; conclusion within the evidence; no citations (unless the venue allows them);
undefined abbreviations removed; keywords (4–6) don't simply repeat the title; word count met.

## 6. Proposals (research and grant)

Reviewers judge **potential and feasibility**: every section answers "why this, why now, why
will it work, why this team?"

**Core structure** (adapt to the funder's template, which always wins):
1. **Title and summary:** problem, aim, approach, outcomes, impact.
2. **Problem and significance:** the gap and its consequences, with evidence.
3. **Aims or objectives:** 2–4 specific, testable aims, each with its hypothesis or question and
   expected outcome. Aims are research questions, not activities ("collect data" is not an aim).
4. **Background and preliminary work:** what makes success plausible; your prior results (real only).
5. **Approach or methods:** per aim, the design, data, analysis, expected results, **potential
   problems and alternative strategies**, and success criteria.
6. **Innovation:** what is new in the concept, method, or application. Be honest about incremental work.
7. **Timeline and milestones:** a Gantt-style table with deliverables.
8. **Team and environment:** expertise mapped to the aims; resources and access.
9. **Budget justification:** every cost tied to an activity.
10. **Impact and dissemination:** who benefits and how; open science; ethics; data management.
11. **Risks and mitigation:** a table of likelihood × impact × mitigation.

**Funder-specific notes:** funders differ in their review criteria. For example, NSF reviews
*Intellectual Merit* and *Broader Impacts*; NIH reviews include *Significance, Innovation,
Approach*; ERC asks for *ground-breaking nature and feasibility*. Always use the current call
text and template, and check page limits and dates, because these change between calls.

**Student and thesis proposals:** title, introduction and problem, literature review (focused),
question or hypotheses, methodology, ethics, timeline, expected contribution, references.

## 7. Theses and dissertations

- The introduction chapter sets out the overarching question, conceptual framework, key
  definitions, contribution claims, and a chapter overview.
- Each chapter gets its own mini-introduction (why this chapter, how it fits) and a bridge to the next.
- Theory chapters must *apply* theories to the thesis question, not just describe them.
- The general discussion synthesises across studies: did the programme answer the question? What
  is the contribution, enumerated? What are the limitations at the thesis level?
- Supervisor feedback: build a change plan as a table (`# | priority | type: clarify/add/cut/
  restructure/fix/expand | location | comment summary | action`) before editing. Execution is
  `editing-and-polish.md`.

## 8. Style rules

- Precise, concrete, economical. Define terms on first use and keep terminology consistent:
  don't vary words for elegance.
- Use active voice where agency matters; passive is fine where the actor is irrelevant.
- First person follows field and venue norms ("we" is common in the sciences).
- Hedge in proportion to the evidence; avoid stacked hedges ("may possibly suggest").
- Match causal language to the design: "associated with" for observational designs, "caused" only
  with causal identification.
- Use "significant" only in the statistical sense; otherwise use "substantial" or "notable".
- Report numbers with units and appropriate precision; define abbreviations; give figures and
  tables self-contained captions.
- Tense: past for what you did and found; present for established knowledge and for what a figure shows.

## 9. Integrity and AI use

- The author is accountable for every claim. AI assistance doesn't make a tool an author.
- Follow the course, institution, or venue's AI-use policy. If disclosure is required, draft it
  from what was actually done, never from assumptions.
- Never generate results, data, ethics statements, consent, funding, conflicts, or author
  contributions. Ask for them or leave a marked placeholder.
- Avoid self-plagiarism and patchwriting. Flag passages that closely track a source for the user
  to rewrite or quote properly.
- Don't send unpublished or confidential manuscripts to external services without the owner's permission.
